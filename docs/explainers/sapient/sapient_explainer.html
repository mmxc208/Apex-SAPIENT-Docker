SAPIENT, explained

What the UK's open standard for networked sensors and effectors is, how its messages flow, what the Apex middleware does with them, and where the Stone Soup tracker could fit. Each section starts with a plain-language summary; open Technical detail for the engineering view.

1

The concept

In plain terms A protected site — an airbase, a summit venue — may use radars, cameras, radio-frequency detectors and microphones from different companies. Normally each speaks its own language, so wiring them together is slow and expensive, and no single sensor sees everything. SAPIENT is a common language. Each sensor does its own thinking and sends short summaries ("drone, 300 m north-east, 80% sure"). A central fusion node combines them into one picture and can tell sensors what to do next ("camera 2, look there") in fractions of a second.

1 · Edge autonomy

Sensors process their own data locally (AI at the edge).

2 · Information, not raw data

Compact summary messages save bandwidth.

3 · Central fusion

One coherent picture, resilient to single-sensor false alarms.

4 · Autonomous tasking

The fusion node re-tasks sensors and effectors at machine speed.

Technical detail
  • Full name: Sensing for Asset Protection with Integrated Electronic Networked Technology. Developed by the UK MOD / Dstl since 2013.
  • Adopted by the MOD as the counter-UAS standard; NATO is adopting it as the basis of a counter-UAS STANREC after the C-UAS Technical Interoperability Exercises. Used to protect leaders at the NATO summit in The Hague (July 2025).
  • Supports ISR sensors (cameras, radar, acoustic, magnetic anomaly…) and soft-kill effectors only (e.g. jammers). Hard-kill effectors are explicitly out of scope; any effector needs an approved safety case (BSI §6.5.8).
  • The standard defines what is exchanged, not how components work inside, protecting vendors' IP.
  • Dstl runs the SAPIENT Standardisation Sub-Committee (S3C) that prepares changes for BSI.
2

Architecture

In plain terms Sensors and effectors ("edge nodes") all connect to one fusion node. Optionally a middleware sits in between, like a post room: it routes, checks and files the messages but should not change them. Bigger systems can be stacked — a local fusion node can pretend to be a single sensor to a higher one.
Technical detail
  • Client/server: the fusion node is the server; edge nodes (and a middleware, if present) are clients (BSI §0.4).
  • Transparency (new in v2): a middleware “has no effect on the content of the messages or the behaviour of the nodes”. Consequently the fusion node sends RegistrationAck and AlertAck, and must keep its own store of registered nodes in case of disconnection.
  • Middleware self-registration (Annex C.3.1): a middleware registers with the fusion node as a node with no modes and no detection reports, and sends regular status reports to keep the channel open. If it has a node_id, it replaces destination_ids as messages pass through.
  • Platforms (§4.10): a MOBILE_NODE (vehicle) or POINTABLE_NODE (gimbal/turret) registers like any node and lists its dependent_nodes; platforms can stack on platforms.
  • Hierarchy (§4.11): a child fusion node registers upward as FUSION_NODE with the aggregated detection and tasking capabilities of its nodes, and reports coverage areas in status messages. Mid-level fusion nodes are client upward and server downward. Hierarchies prevent loops that would distort fusion weightings.
2a · new in v2

Platforms: sensors on things that point or move

In plain terms Sensors are often bolted onto something else: a gimbal that swivels, or a vehicle or drone that moves. Turning the gimbal turns every camera on it; driving the vehicle moves everything aboard. So in v2 the gimbal or vehicle joins the network as a node of its own and says “these sensors ride on me”. The fusion node then knows to steer the gimbal (not the camera) to look somewhere, and to ask the camera (not the gimbal) to be more sensitive.

Register the platform

The platform registers like any node with node_type POINTABLE_NODE (gimbal, turret) or MOBILE_NODE (ground, air or sea vehicle), and lists the UUIDs of nodes it carries in dependent_nodes.

Split the commands

Platforms declare movement commands (LOOK_AT, MOVE_TO, PATROL, FOLLOW); dependents declare intrinsic ones (DETECTION_THRESHOLD, MODE_CHANGE…).

Follow an object

A dependent sensor reports a detection with an object_id; the fusion node sends FOLLOW with follow_object_id = that ULID; the platform decides how to follow.

Report movement

Platform status values: Stationary, Transiting, Slewing, Following, Stuck, Drifting. Sensors can report Node_Moving / Node_Stopped / Node_Paused.

Technical detail — rules (BSI Flex 335 v2.0 §4.10)
  • Scope: the fusion node is not on the platform; only edge nodes are. If a fusion node rides on a platform, the hierarchy rules of §4.11 apply instead (see 2b).
  • Stacking: complex dependencies are platforms on platforms (BSI Fig. 4): the mobile node declares the pointable node and edge node 3; the pointable node declares edge nodes 1 and 2.
  • Default: a platform that declares neither mobile nor pointable is assumed static.
  • Commands (Table 58): MOVE_TO takes a LocationList — one point is the destination, several are waypoints. PATROL repeats a LocationList route until a new command. LOOK_AT takes a LocationList or RangeBearingCone. FOLLOW takes a FollowObject (Table 59: follow_object_id, mandatory); only one target is supported in v2. A platform without Follow support simply doesn't declare it — the fusion node can approximate it with repeated Move_To / Look_At.
  • Regions (Table 51): MOBILE_NODE_GO_AREA (stay within) and MOBILE_NODE_NO_GO_AREA (do not enter), alongside AREA_OF_INTEREST, IGNORE and BOUNDARY.
  • Platform ↔ dependents interface is implementation-specific: dependents may need the platform's location, velocity, roll, pitch and yaw to correct their observations; a platform supporting Follow may need detection locations from its dependents. BSI doesn't standardise this link.
  • Bearings: RangeBearingDatum.PLATFORM (north = platform heading) exists, but BSI notes its definition is deferred to the next edition.
  • Proto fields: Registration.dependent_nodes (repeated string, field 8); node types 18–20 in registration.proto; follow.proto is new in v2.
2b · new in v2

Hierarchies: a fusion node can be someone else's sensor

In plain terms One fusion node can only handle so many sensors and so much area. v2 lets you build a tree: each local fusion node looks after its own patch (or its own vehicle), and a higher fusion node combines the patches. The neat part is that the higher node doesn't need anything new — each local fusion node introduces itself as if it were one big sensor with all the abilities of the sensors beneath it.
Child capability levelWhat the child fusion node must do
No hierarchical capabilitySends no registration; no communication upward
Supports detectionRegisters with the parent, declaring the combined detection capabilities of all its edge nodes, and sends detection reports upward
Supports taskingAlso declares the combined tasks and modes of its edge nodes, and accepts tasks from the parent
Technical detail — rules (BSI Flex 335 v2.0 §4.11)
  • Level-agnostic: to its parent, a child fusion node “registers, sends detection reports and is taskable as if it were an edge node”, so the tree can have any number of levels.
  • Node type: the child registers with NODE_TYPE_FUSION_NODE (value 20). A child fusion node that physically rides on a platform reports both FUSION_NODE and MOBILE_NODE / POINTABLE_NODE, and declares which taskings it supports.
  • Coverage: Registration.reporting_region (field 9) tells the parent the region the child reports from. StatusReport.coverage became repeated (field 12) so a child can report the total coverage of its edge nodes, and must update it as edge nodes (or lower fusion nodes) go on- and off-line.
  • Tasking: the parent sends commands to the child; the child “autonomously determines how best to satisfy the request(s)”, e.g. by commanding its platform or edge nodes.
  • Connections: the top fusion node is a server; a mid-level node opens two channels — client to its parent, server to its children; edge nodes are clients.
  • Registration order: bottom-up (edge nodes first) is preferred, not mandatory; it avoids repeated coverage updates.
  • Why a tree: GOV.UK notes that the hierarchy prevents loops in the information architecture that would distort weightings in fusion (the same detection counted twice).

History of the standard

In plain terms SAPIENT started as a Dstl document using XML. In 2023 it switched to a compact binary format (Google Protocol Buffers) and was then published by the British Standards Institution as BSI Flex 335. Version 2.0 (March 2024) is current.

Message lifecycle

In plain terms A sensor introduces itself (Registration), gets a “welcome” (RegistrationAck), then keeps saying “I'm alive” (StatusReport) and reports what it sees (DetectionReport). The fusion node can give it jobs (Task), which it confirms (TaskAck). When it shuts down it says goodbye.

5

Wire format

In plain terms Every message is an “envelope” with a timestamp, the sender's ID, optionally the recipient's ID, and exactly one kind of content. Because the binary format doesn't mark where one message ends, each is preceded by a 4-byte length. 
TCP stream:  [len: uint32 LE][SapientMessage bytes][len][SapientMessage bytes]...

message SapientMessage {
  google.protobuf.Timestamp timestamp = 1;  // M — time of the CONTENT (e.g. when detected)
  string node_id        = 2;                // M — sender UUID v4
  string destination_id = 3;                // C — recipient UUID (set on acks, tasks, errors)
  oneof content {                           // M — exactly one:
    Registration registration = 4;  RegistrationAck registration_ack = 5;
    StatusReport status_report = 6; DetectionReport detection_report = 7;
    Task task = 8;                  TaskAck task_ack = 9;
    Alert alert = 10;               AlertAck alert_ack = 11;
    Error error = 12;
  }
  string additional_information = 13;       // D — new in v2
}
Technical detail — gotchas
  • The length prefix is little-endian and excludes itself; this differs from Google's usual varint-delimited convention (BSI §4.2 Note 2).
  • Enum value 0 is UNSPECIFIED: a mandatory enum left at 0 is an error; a discretionary one is just “not set”.
  • Protobuf drops zero-valued scalars, so a real 0 (e.g. azimuth 0) in a mandatory field needs care.
  • Omitting z in a Location or elevation in a RangeBearing means “ambiguous”, not zero.
  • The .proto files are normative in practice: BSI advises all components use the same files. They are in SAPIENT-Proto-Files/ and vendored in Apex as sapient_msg/, annotated with validation options (is_mandatory, is_uuid, is_ulid…).

Apex middleware

In plain terms Apex is Dstl's free middleware (written in Python by Roke, Apache 2.0). It's the post room: it knows which socket belongs to which sensor, so a task goes only to the right sensor instead of being shouted to all of them. It checks messages against the standard, translates between old and new versions, keeps a full recording you can replay later, and remembers each sensor's introduction so a fusion node that starts late still finds out about everyone.
Port typeConnectsBehaviour
ChildSensors / effectorsFirst message must be a Registration; afterwards everything is forwarded to all Peers. Wrong node_id → Error back; node_id taken over by another socket → dropped.
PeerFusion nodeMessages with destination_id go only to that Child; messages without one are “high-level” and go to Parents.
ParentHigher C2 / another ApexReceives high-level messages; with forwardAll, everything (plus the cache on connect).
RecorderAny feedStored only, not routed.

Practical rule: keep every node that uses platforms or hierarchies, and the fusion nodes that consume them, on BSI v2 end to end.

Technical detail — other observations from the source
  • SAPIENT ports have no authentication or TLS, and trio.serve_tcp(handler, port) listens on all interfaces.
  • v1↔v2 translators catch exceptions and return an empty message; v1→v2 fills config_data with placeholder manufacturer/model.
  • With several Peer ports configured, the last one's format is used for all.

Where Stone Soup fits

In plain terms Stone Soup is Dstl's open-source toolkit for tracking moving objects (filters that turn noisy detections into smooth tracks). It is exactly the kind of engine a fusion node needs, but it doesn't speak SAPIENT yet — someone has to write a small “bridge” between Apex and Stone Soup.
Technical detail — bridge design notes
  1. Connect to Apex as a Peer: TCP client, read 4-byte LE length-prefixed SapientMessages (optionally register first; Apex's allowPeerRegistration defaults to true).
  2. Ingest with a custom Stone Soup DetectionReader: DetectionReport → Detection. Use the message timestamp as measurement time; build each sensor's measurement model from its Registration (coordinate system, geometric error) and its StatusReport node location. Range/bearing sensors need a polar (bearing–range) model.
  3. Track with standard Stone Soup components (predictor, updater, data associator, initiator, deleter).
  4. Publish fused tracks as DetectionReports from the fusion node's own node_id, without destination_id (so Apex forwards them to Parents), listing source detections in associated_detection. Only the ENU velocity frame exists in v2.
  5. Task (optional): send Task messages with a destination_id; Apex delivers them to that one sensor.

Stone Soup today has readers for Kafka, CBOR, YAML, HDF5, AIS and OpenSky, and writers for Kafka, CBOR, YAML — nothing for SAPIENT or raw TCP. No repo in this folder references the other.
